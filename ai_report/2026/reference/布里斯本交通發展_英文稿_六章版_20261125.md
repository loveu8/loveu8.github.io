## 1 Public transport today: what runs, and how many trips a year?

Public transport in South East Queensland is planned by Translink as one network of four modes: bus (including Brisbane Metro, which started in 2025), rail, light rail and ferry; light rail is the Gold Coast's G:link, outside Brisbane City. In 2025–26 it carried about 214 million trips, 13% more than in 2018–19, before the pandemic. In the 2016 Census, before the pandemic, about one in ten people who travelled to work in the 11 local government areas used public transport (see Chapter 2). The cards below show how many routes each mode has and how many trips it carries. [C-03, C-17, C-34, C-35]

Three points to keep in mind when reading the cards. First, growth is measured against the pre-pandemic year; counted from 2019–20 it would be about 40%, but that year was affected by the pandemic from March 2020, and the low point was 113 million in 2021–22. Second, TMR states that 50-cent fares from 2024–25 increased demand on every mode. Third, the 12 rail lines are taken from Translink's list of all train timetables; the list dropped one line for a time and in 2026 switched to T1 to T6 groups, so changes in the count come from how lines are listed and named; no new rail line opened in these six years, and Cross River Rail, now under construction, is expected to carry its first passengers in 2029. [C-06, C-34, C-35]

With more people travelling, did the number of bus routes grow too? Counting each bus route in the local government area that holds most of its stops: from April 2020 to October 2026 the total went from 431 to 435 routes, with a peak of 446 in March 2024, almost unchanged. Brisbane City went from 227 to 217, around the time of the 2025 Metro and bus network changes, but this report has not checked the reason route by route. Ipswich, with the highest five-year population growth, went from 15 to 19 routes, and Logan from 29 to 31; no route is based mainly in Scenic Rim. The route count only counts routes, not their length or frequency, so more services on one route do not change the number; a route that crosses areas is also counted in one area only, and about half of the routes in Logan and Redland cross areas. [C-01, C-36]

**Takeaway.** In 2025–26, public transport in South East Queensland carried about 214 million trips, about 13% more than before the pandemic; most of the growth was on buses (up about 14%), while rail grew only about 3%. Over the same period the number of bus routes barely changed and no new rail line opened, so the extra passengers are travelling on a network of about the same size. The population grew too; whether trips kept up once population is taken into account is the subject of the next chapter. [C-34, C-35, C-36]

## 2 Network and population: more people, so how do they get to work?

### Where the population is growing and where jobs are

From June 2020 to June 2025, the estimated resident population of the 11 local government areas rose from about 3.60 million to 4.01 million, an increase of 11.2%, or about 400,000 people. Growth was fastest on the outskirts: the two highest five-year growth rates were Ipswich (18.3%) and Logan (17.4%), and the areas with the highest growth rate in the state in 2024–25 listed by the Australian Bureau of Statistics, Ripley, Morayfield and Greenbank–North Maclean, are on the outskirts of Ipswich, Moreton Bay and Logan, respectively. The Queensland Government Statistician's Office 2025 edition projects about 4.42 million people by 2031 in the medium series, with the low and high series between 4.32 and 4.53 million. Looking further ahead, from 2021 to 2046 Ipswich's population roughly doubles (from about 230,000 to 480,000), Logan grows by about two-thirds and Brisbane City by about a third. These are projections, not actual estimates. [C-01, C-16, C-27]

Jobs, by contrast, are most concentrated in the inner city. In the 2021 Census, 12.3% of the roughly 1.19 million employed people whose workplace was in Greater Brisbane worked in the Brisbane City area alone; four of the top five areas are around the inner city (Brisbane City, South Brisbane, Fortitude Valley and Newstead–Bowen Hills), together about one-fifth (19.4%). These data cover only the ABS Greater Brisbane statistical area, which excludes the Gold Coast and includes only a small part of the Sunshine Coast on Bribie Island, and Census day fell during the pandemic. People living on the outskirts and working in the inner city could concentrate peak inbound demand on a few main corridors; however, this report has no origin–destination data from home to work, so this is an inference, not an observation. [C-28, C-29]

### How today's network came about

The major projects covered here were added to the existing network over the past thirty years, and for several of them the government of the day cited population and commuting pressure. The Gold Coast railway opened in stages from 1996 and reached Robina in 1998; in 1992 then Prime Minister Keating said development in the Gold Coast–Brisbane region had taken place without appropriate public transport. The South East Busway began regular services at the end of April 2001, on the section that includes the stop sampled in Chapter 3: the minister said at the time that a peak trip from Eight Mile Plains to the city could take up to an hour, against about 18 minutes on the busway. Airtrain opened in May the same year, linking the airport, the city and the Gold Coast. Rail later reached Springfield (2013) and the Redcliffe Peninsula (2016). [C-15]

The 18 minutes in 2001 is a minister's statement, while the M1's scheduled 22 minutes in 2026 is a stop-to-stop time. The two are measured differently and cannot show whether the trip became faster or slower. [C-15, C-08]

### The peak: most people drive, and inbound trains are crowded too

In the 2016 Census, before the pandemic, about eight in ten people in the 11 local government areas who travelled to work went by car only, as driver or passenger, and about one in ten used public transport; even in Brisbane City, where the public transport share is highest, it was under one in five. Census day in 2021 fell during the pandemic, and the share of all employed people working from home rose from 5% to 17%, so the shares cannot be compared with 2016 to measure change. From 2020 to 2025, registered passenger cars increased by 14.2%, faster than the population's 11.2%; passenger cars per 1,000 people rose from 591 to 607. Registrations are not the same as the number of people driving each day. [C-17, C-18]

As the population grew, did public transport use grow with it? Dividing a year's trips on the whole Translink network in South East Queensland by the mid-year population of the 11 local government areas (years run from July to June): before the pandemic each person made about 53 to 55 trips a year, roughly flat, meaning trips grew in step with the population. The figure fell sharply during the pandemic, to 30.7 in 2021–22. From 2018–19 to 2024–25 the population grew from about 3.50 million to about 3.97 million, up about 13%, while trips in a year rose from about 190 million to 193 million, up only about 2%; trips per person therefore fell from 54.2 to 48.7, about one-tenth fewer, even though 50-cent fares were first trialled and then made permanent that year. In 2025–26 trips rose by about another 11%, to 214 million, but the population for that year will not be published until 2027, so trips per person cannot yet be calculated, and it cannot be said that trips have caught up. More trips do not mean that services and seats are sufficient. [C-33]

Only about one in ten commuters uses public transport, yet inbound trains are already crowded in the peak. Translink's Service Capacity Tracker forecasts space on board in three levels for every line, stop and 15-minute period. In the data last updated in June 2025, about one-fifth (18.7%) of stop-and-time cells on inbound trains between 7 and 9 am on weekdays were marked "limited space", busiest around 7:30 and none after 9 am. The most crowded lines were Springfield (46%), Redcliffe (31%) and Gold Coast (30%), which come in from Ipswich, the Redcliffe Peninsula in Moreton Bay, and Logan and the Gold Coast; Ipswich and Logan had the highest five-year population growth. The Airport, Beenleigh and Doomben lines had no "limited space" cells. These are official forecasts, the thresholds for each level are not published, and the data predate Metro, so they show where trains are crowded but not how much more crowded they are than before. [C-37]

A TMR performance snapshot shows that in 2022/23, on sampled sections of state-controlled roads in South East Queensland, travel time per 10 km was 30 seconds longer in the morning peak and 39 seconds longer in the afternoon peak than the average of the previous four years (2018/19 to 2021/22); this baseline includes pandemic years. Separately, a 2022 travel survey compared with 2017–2019 found that the public transport mode share in South East Queensland fell by 1.9 percentage points, while average commuting time to work fell by 9.8%; both comparisons span the pandemic, and the snapshot gives no reason. In an advocacy article by RACQ, a motoring member organisation, citing its own annual travel speeds report, more than 70% of about 512 km of key road sections in Brisbane and the Gold Coast were slower in the 2025 peak than in 2019. Results for travel to work from the 2026 Census are expected in October 2027 and can provide a post-pandemic baseline before these rail projects open. [C-19, C-20, C-32]

In this report these figures are the demand background that public transport has to face; road projects themselves are examined in the next report.

### Off-peak and weekends: some lines still run infrequently

Clayfield, on the Doomben line, has direct trains to the city. In samples taken from 10:00 to 15:00 on 21 October 2026 (Wednesday) and 24 October (Saturday), the longest gaps between direct inbound trains were 30 and 60 minutes respectively; buses 300 and 322 also run nearby. The rail projects checked here have announced no weekend service increase for this station. In June 2026, across the Citytrain network 95.16% of peak services ran on time and 99.84% of scheduled services were delivered across the day (adjusted, as the official method does, to exclude events such as storms that are beyond control); network-wide figures do not represent actual waiting times at individual stations. [C-02, C-12, C-14]

**Takeaway.** Between 1996 and 2016 these projects were added to the existing network, and for the Gold Coast railway and the busway the government of the day cited population and commuting pressure. The population is still growing quickly, growth is fastest on the outskirts, and jobs are most concentrated in the inner city; most commuters travelled by car only, as driver or passenger (2016 Census), cars are increasing faster than people, peak travel times rose on sampled sections of state-controlled roads, and the 2022 travel survey also showed a lower public transport share than in 2017–2019. Public transport trips per person were roughly flat before the pandemic and were still about one-tenth lower in 2024–25; trips rose by about another tenth in 2025–26, for which the population is not yet published. Each person is not travelling more, yet inbound trains are already crowded in the weekday morning peak, most of all on the lines from Ipswich, Moreton Bay, Logan and the Gold Coast. What has already been done about these pressures is covered in Chapter 3; what is being built, and how much capacity it adds, in Chapter 4. [C-01, C-15 to C-19, C-27 to C-29, C-33, C-37]

## 3 Reforms already in place: what changed, and how many more people did it carry?

Brisbane Metro is an electric bus service running on the busways. M2 began in January 2025, and M1 began in June that year together with the second stage of bus route changes; 50-cent fares became permanent from February 2025 on eligible Translink services, excluding Airtrain. [C-03, C-04]

Take Eight Mile Plains, a stop on the South East Busway. Comparing two sampled Wednesdays, 14 May 2025 and 21 October 2026, the trunk services from this stop to Cultural Centre rose across the day from 99 on the old 111 to 182 on the M1. At 7 and 8 am both ran every 5 minutes; route 555 also serves the stop, and in the 7 am hour it went from 5 services to 4. The M1 uses 24.4-metre Metro vehicles (the council says about twice the length of a standard bus), and officially each vehicle comfortably carries 150 passengers; at 12 services an hour in the peak, that works out to about 1,800 passengers an hour towards the city at that comfortable capacity. No public data shows which vehicles ran each old 111 service, so how many more people can be carried in the peak cannot be calculated. Between 9 am and 4 pm, services went from 4 an hour to 12. Compared with Clayfield in Chapter 2, the more frequent off-peak service is a change on this corridor, not the norm across the city. Counting whole routes in both directions across the day, the old 111 and 160 together ran 328 services and the M1 runs 364. [C-03, C-21]

The change did not add services everywhere. The old 160 started from the Garden City depot stops (MacGregor St, two stops) and ran directly to the city; the M1 does not serve these stops. On the sampled Wednesdays, direct services from here to Cultural Centre fell from 149 to 93, leaving only route 150; some former 160 passengers may need to transfer to the M1 at Upper Mt Gravatt. [C-22]

Card records also changed. In two Mays, both after 50-cent fares became permanent, card taps at boarding with go card and contactless bank cards were 193,986 on the old 111 and 160 in 2025 and 382,655 on the M1 in 2026. This does not mean the number of people travelling doubled: each transfer adds another record, and the network was changed at the same time. Nor does it mean fewer people are driving; the data cannot separate new passengers from existing passengers travelling more. [C-04]

**Takeaway.** Since the change, off-peak services on this corridor have become more frequent; comparing the two Mays, card taps at boarding on the old 111, 160 and the M1 also rose, but how much comes from the change itself cannot be separated. The inbound morning-peak frequency did not change, and services now use Metro vehicles, but no public data describes the old 111 vehicles, so the added capacity cannot be calculated; direct services from some stops actually fell. The large increases in peak capacity identified in this report come mainly from rail. [C-03, C-04, C-21, C-22, C-23]

## 4 Projects under construction: how much capacity will they add?

The services and capacity below are official expectations. The Queensland Audit Office lists estimated delivery years provided by the delivery agencies. An estimated delivery year is the year the delivery agency expects the project to be complete and/or operational; the listed year does not distinguish between the two. For Cross River Rail the audit office's table also shows 2029, and the delivery authority separately states that this is when first passenger services are expected.

**Cross River Rail.** About 10.2 km of new railway from Dutton Park to Bowen Hills, including about 5.9 km of twin tunnels and four underground stations. The whole project, together with associated works, is estimated at about A$19.041 billion, of which about A$12.4 billion is for the design, construction and maintenance of the tunnels and underground stations; first passenger services are officially expected in 2029. It is officially said to be able to run up to 24 trains in each direction, without stating whether this is per hour or in the peak, and there is no pre-project figure to compare with. It is the second rail river crossing into the inner city, which is where jobs are most concentrated, and it is officially described as the core project that makes other rail improvements more feasible. [C-06, C-13, C-23, C-28]

**Southbound: Logan and Gold Coast Faster Rail.** Logan, which it serves, had the second-fastest five-year population growth of the local government areas (17.4%). About 20 km from Kuraby to Beenleigh goes from two tracks to four, funded half each by the federal and state governments, with a main project budget of A$5.75 billion and an estimated delivery year of 2031. Before the upgrade, express and all-stops trains share one pair of tracks, with a maximum of 12 trains an hour, and before the pandemic peak express trains were already standing-room only by Altandi. After quadruplication the two types of service run separately; in the business case evaluated by Infrastructure Australia, the federal infrastructure adviser, operational modelling expects "turn-up-and-go" peak services every five minutes. However, the evaluation does not say which stations this applies to or give the track capacity after the upgrade, so the added capacity cannot be calculated. Today, arriving at Loganlea at 8 am means a 4-minute wait for the 08:04 train, then 40 minutes on board, reaching Central at 08:44; stopping patterns and timetables will need to be checked again after completion. [C-01, C-06, C-09, C-13, C-23]

**Northbound: Sunshine Coast, The Wave Stage 1.** The Sunshine Coast population grew by 12.9% in five years. Stage 1 runs from Beerwah to Caloundra, with a budget of A$5.5 billion funded half each by the federal and state governments, and an estimated delivery year of 2032. In the 2024 business case evaluated by the same body, the Stage 1 option plans 6 trains an hour at opening, up to 8 in the long term, and 10 to 12 in the peak during events. The demand model in the same business case simulated a full railway from Beerwah all the way to Maroochydore; the current plan has since changed to rail to Birtinya followed by a Metro-style service to the airport, so the figures below are for reference only. In that scenario, the public transport mode share on the Sunshine Coast rises only from 1.4% to 1.9% by 2046; in the same year, private vehicles account for 87.3% of trips produced on the Sunshine Coast with this railway and 87.7% without it. [C-01, C-07, C-13, C-23]

**A corridor the projects do not reach.** From Indooroopilly, catching route 432 at 8 am gives a 16-minute direct trip on board to the University of Queensland. The projects checked include no service increase for this pair of stops, and direct improvement is expected to be limited. Studies are still assessing whether to build four Brisbane Metro extension corridors. [C-07, C-10]

Ipswich, projected to grow fastest, also has no rail extension among the projects checked in this report: none of the five ETCS digital signalling sections or the four directions of Metro extension head towards Ipswich. The project list here was chosen by selection rules and is not every plan, so this report can only say there is none within the scope checked, not that the government has no plans. [C-27, C-30]

**Takeaway.** Increases in peak capacity rest mainly on these railways, but for now there are only official expectations, not actual results. On how much the public transport share of trips could rise, this report found only the 2024 business case forecast for a full Beerwah to Maroochydore railway, and the forecast share stays low (1.9% by 2046); no comparable forecast was found for Cross River Rail or Logan and Gold Coast Faster Rail. [C-06, C-07, C-23]

## 5 Against 2032: which projects will be ready for the Games?

Stage 2 of the Gold Coast light rail, G:link, is a local precedent: it connects to Helensvale station, and the state government called it key infrastructure supporting the 2018 Commonwealth Games. The pre-opening announcement scheduled passenger services to start on 18 December 2017, ahead of the original timeline, and a state government statement during the Games confirmed that Stage 2 began service in December 2017, before the Games in April 2018. However, it was only 7.3 km with a total cost of A$420 million; the rail projects that now have to meet a deadline are of a different order, and it cannot be inferred that "it made it last time, so it will this time". [C-25]

Games travel demand is on another scale. Official forecasts put Games-time travel at more than 2 million trips a day, on top of residents' everyday commuting. The bid target is for at least 90% of trips to venues during the Games to be by public transport, walking or cycling. On a normal day, about 8–10% of all trips in Brisbane's urban area are by public transport. The two figures cover different trips and modes, cannot be compared directly, and do not show how many people who usually drive would need to switch. The more direct evidence is GIICA's corridor-by-corridor assessment: in Brisbane's eastern corridor, demand of about 51,000 passengers a day exceeds current bus capacity; on the Gold Coast, rail demand of about 193,000 a day far exceeds existing capacity; on the Sunshine Coast, demand of about 126,000 a day far exceeds current bus capacity, and GIICA recommends rail to meet it. [C-24]

GIICA lists nine transport programs that must be delivered before the 2032 Games. In its response to each recommendation, the state government accepted all nine, noting for each that funding opportunities will be required to progress it; the 2032 Delivery Plan separately names The Wave, Logan and Gold Coast Faster Rail, Cross River Rail and the Brisbane Metro expansion for acceleration. Against the delivery agencies' estimated years listed by the Queensland Audit Office (for the projects checked individually in this report): [C-24, C-26]

- **Expected to carry passengers before the Games on current estimates**: Cross River Rail, passenger services in 2029.
- **Tight, with almost no margin**: Logan and Gold Coast Faster Rail in 2031 and The Wave Stage 1 in 2032. In evaluating the 2024 business case, Infrastructure Australia also noted that for The Wave Stage 1 to operate for the Games, it must reach practical completion by 2031, leaving very little margin for testing and delays.
- **Cannot be judged**: The Wave Stage 2 has planning and early works, but funding for main construction and a completion date are not yet confirmed; for other items on the list, such as bus priority corridors and the ETCS expansion, this report did not find completion dates item by item. The Games Route Network road upgrades on GIICA's list are left to the next report.

**Takeaway.** Of the projects checked in this report, one is on track by current estimates, two are tight and the rest cannot be judged. GIICA assesses that Games demand in the eastern corridor, on the Gold Coast and on the Sunshine Coast exceeds existing capacity; whether the railways are finished on time is one of the key conditions, but the eastern bus corridor and other items GIICA names also affect whether this commitment can be met. [C-24, C-26]

## 6 Can the money hold up?

On construction, Logan and Gold Coast Faster Rail and The Wave Stage 1 are both funded half each by the federal and state governments, and both have cumulative spending reported by the Queensland Audit Office. The delivery budget for the original Brisbane Metro project was about A$1.65 billion, funded mainly by the council and the federal government, with cumulative spending of A$1.54 billion to the end of June 2025. Funders, purposes and amounts for the eight projects and policies are in the [figure notes](data/brisbane-transport/stage-4-figures-six-chapter.md). [C-13]

On operations, the state budget's general public transport subsidy for South East Queensland is an estimated actual of about A$2.787 billion in 2025–26 and a budget of about A$2.946 billion in 2026–27. TMR's departmental fare revenue fell from about A$324.7 million in 2023–24 to about A$88.1 million in 2025–26, a period in which 50-cent fares were first trialled and then made permanent; the state budget separately lists A$1.522 billion over five years for permanent 50-cent fares. Departmental fare revenue and the operating subsidy cover different things and cannot simply be subtracted. Some project totals already include future operations and maintenance: the Queensland Audit Office lists Cross River Rail's core project cost in December 2024 as A$10.6 billion, including A$2.6 billion for future operations and maintenance (without stating how many years it covers); the state's A$9.5 billion contribution to the Queensland Train Manufacturing Program includes 20 years of operations and maintenance. These are estimates folded into project totals, not year-by-year or line-by-line operating costs. Operating costs and annual maintenance spending for the M1 and M2 cannot be identified from public data. Road construction and maintenance funding is left to the next report. [C-11, C-13, C-31]

**Takeaway.** For most construction funding, the funders and spending are already documented; operations rely on state subsidy, and fare revenue fell sharply while 50-cent fares were in place. This report found no official estimate of year-by-year, line-by-line operating costs once the new railways open; whether the money holds up depends on future state budgets. [C-11, C-13, C-31]

## 7 Back to the core question: can it keep up?

**What has already happened.** The population grew by about 400,000 in five years, growth is fastest on the outskirts, and jobs are most concentrated in the inner city; most commuters travelled by car only, as driver or passenger (2016 Census), cars are increasing faster than people, and peak travel times rose on sampled sections of state-controlled roads. [C-01, C-17, C-18, C-19, C-27, C-28]

**What can be checked.** Reforms are in place: 50-cent fares are permanent, and Metro has made off-peak services at Eight Mile Plains more frequent, but the inbound morning-peak frequency did not change, the added capacity from Metro vehicles cannot be calculated, and direct services from some stops fell. The progress and budgets of the rail projects can be tracked. [C-03, C-04, C-06, C-07, C-13, C-21, C-22]

**A conditional judgement.** The large increases in peak capacity identified in this report come mainly from rail projects. On current estimates Cross River Rail can carry passengers before the Games; the estimated delivery years for Logan and Gold Coast Faster Rail and The Wave Stage 1 sit right up against 2032. Back to the Eight Mile Plains stop in Chapter 3: it is on the busway, and none of these railways pass it. In the data checked, there is no peak service increase planned for this stop, and the Metro extensions are still being studied. Results for travel to work from the 2026 Census are expected in October 2027 and can provide a post-pandemic baseline before these railways open. Operating records after opening and later censuses can show changes in patronage and in how people travel to work; but confirming whether people who used to drive have switched would need a survey that follows the same people over time. [C-06, C-07, C-21, C-23, C-26, C-32]
