# Brisbane transport: what changes for everyday trips?

Research draft | 20 November 2026. Project status is reported as of the research cut-off. Timetable examples apply to the dates shown. Claim IDs link to the [claims register](data/brisbane-transport/claims.csv), and source IDs can be looked up in the [source register](data/brisbane-transport/sources.csv).

## Summary

**Fares and timetables have changed. Who gets a quicker trip from the new rail projects will depend on the timetables and how services run after opening.** [C-03, C-04, C-06, C-07]

- **Already running.** Brisbane Metro electric bus services M2 and M1 began in January and June 2025 respectively. The 50-cent fare became permanent in February 2025. [C-03, C-04]
- **Under development.** Cross River Rail expects to carry its first passengers in 2029. The Queensland Audit Office lists estimated completion in 2031 for the southern rail upgrade and 2032 for The Wave Stage 1. [C-06, C-07]
- **What changed for a commuter?** Both the old 111 and M1 timetables allowed 22 minutes on board between Eight Mile Plains and Cultural Centre on the sampled dates. [C-08]
- **Who pays?** The federal and Queensland governments each fund half of the southern rail upgrade and The Wave Stage 1. Queensland separately budgeted about A$2.946 billion in 2026–27 for general public transport operations in South East Queensland. [C-11, C-13]

## 1. Where is the region growing, and how does the network serve it?

The population of the 11 local government areas covered in this analysis grew from about 3.60 million in June 2020 to 4.01 million in June 2025, up 11.2%. Logan grew by 17.4% and Ipswich by 18.3%. In a separate ABS geography, the 12-month average number of employed Greater Brisbane residents rose from about 1.48 million to 1.52 million and then 1.55 million in the years ending September 2024, 2025 and 2026. Census workplace data help identify destinations such as the city centre, universities and hospitals. [C-01]

Clayfield is a station on the Doomben line with direct trains into the city. From 10 am to 3 pm, the longest scheduled gap between direct city-bound trains was 30 minutes on Wednesday 21 October 2026 and 60 minutes on Saturday 24 October. The nearby 300 and 322 buses also run towards the city. Which option works depends on the destination, the walk to the stop and the time the passenger gets there. [C-02]

### Does the scheduled service actually run?

In June 2026, Citytrain reported **95.16% peak punctuality** and **99.84% all-day service delivery** across the network. Punctuality measures arrivals within the allowed window at designated stations. Service delivery measures how much of the timetable ran and accounts for partial and full cancellations. Both published measures are adjusted for force majeure events. [C-14]

The 60 minutes at Clayfield is a scheduled interval. A cancellation or delay could extend the wait. The two percentages describe the Citytrain network. Station-level operating records would be needed to establish what happened at Clayfield on the day. [C-02, C-14]

**Takeaway.** Population and employment provide context for demand. To understand waiting times, start with the timetable and check whether services ran and arrived on time. [C-01, C-02, C-14]

## 2. What has changed so far?

Metro M2 began operating in January 2025. M1 and the second stage of the new bus network followed in June. Brisbane Metro uses electric buses on existing busways. The old 111 and 160 had 328 scheduled trips between them on Wednesday 14 May 2025, while M1 had 364 on Wednesday 21 October 2026. Both counts cover the full day and both directions. The original Metro delivery program was budgeted at about A$1.65 billion, funded primarily by Brisbane City Council and the Australian Government. The Queensland Audit Office reported A$1.54 billion in cumulative spending to 30 June 2025. [C-03, C-13]

For May 2025 on the old 111 and 160, and May 2026 on the M1, the recorded go card and contactless bank card boardings were 193,986 and 382,655 respectively. Both months fell under permanent 50-cent fares. A transfer can generate another boarding record, and the bus network was also changing. The 50-cent fare became permanent in February 2025 and applies to eligible Translink services, excluding Airtrain. [C-04]

The Kangaroo Point walking and cycling bridge opened in December 2024. Brisbane City Council and the Australian Government contributed funding, and the opening announcement put delivery costs at A$288 million. The northern section of Coomera Connector Stage 1 opened first. Work continued on the central and southern sections. [C-05, C-13]

**Takeaway.** Timetables and fares have changed, and so have recorded boardings. Evidence with consistent coverage is still needed to establish changes in passenger numbers and door-to-door travel times. [C-03, C-04]

## 3. Which parts of the railway are being upgraded?

Cross River Rail adds about 10.2 km of railway between Dutton Park and Bowen Hills, including about 5.9 km of twin tunnels and four underground stations. The broader program estimate is A$19.041 billion. The state delivery authority, Queensland Rail and other parties fund different work packages, and the delivery authority's capital budget includes private financing. The project has entered systems integration and testing. The first passenger services are expected in 2029. [C-06, C-13]

Further south, about 20 km of railway between Kuraby and Beenleigh is being upgraded from two tracks to four. Existing passenger services still run on the corridor. The Australian and Queensland governments are each funding half of the A$5.75 billion main project budget, and cumulative spending has been reported. The works are expected to increase capacity. Passenger services still depend on signalling, stations, rolling stock and the operating timetable. Queensland estimates its wider train manufacturing program at A$9.5 billion, including construction and 20 years of operations and maintenance. Capital costs are reported separately. [C-06, C-13]

**Takeaway.** The new river crossing and extra southern tracks provide infrastructure for future service increases. When trains run and which stations get more services depend on operating decisions. [C-06]

## 4. Where is the network planned to expand?

On the Sunshine Coast, The Wave is planned in three stages. The first stage would connect the existing railway at Beerwah to Caloundra, then extend to Birtinya, where passengers would transfer to a Metro-style bus service to the airport. Stage 1 has a A$5.5 billion budget, split equally between the Australian and Queensland governments. Cumulative spending has been reported. Stages 2 and 3 share planning and procurement preparation funding. Stage 2 construction remains subject to approvals and funding. Four Brisbane Metro extension corridors are also being studied under a A$150 million business case program. [C-07, C-13]

**Takeaway.** The Wave Stage 1 has a construction budget and reported spending. Stage 2 still faces approval and funding conditions. The Brisbane Metro extensions are being assessed. [C-07]

## 5. Which part of a daily trip might change?

Each timetable example starts at 8 am at the boarding point. Only the south-side trip has a before-and-after comparison, using the old 111 on 14 May 2025 and M1 on 21 October 2026. The Logan and west-side examples both use the latter date. Waiting times apply to arrival at the stop at exactly 8 am. The [journey table](data/brisbane-transport/journeys.csv) records scheduled times from the boarding point to arrival. [C-08, C-09, C-10]

- **Eight Mile Plains to Cultural Centre.** The old 111 and the M1 each had a 22-minute ride on the sampled dates. Arriving at 8 am meant catching the old 111 immediately or waiting one minute for the M1. [C-08]
- **Loganlea to Central.** On the October 2026 sample date, the wait was four minutes, followed by 40 minutes on the Gold Coast line train, from 8:04 to 8:44 am. The southern rail upgrade could affect station and service arrangements. Future stops and frequencies remain to be confirmed. [C-09]
- **Indooroopilly to the University of Queensland's St Lucia campus.** On the sampled day, the 432 left at 8 am and arrived 16 minutes later. The projects reviewed give no specific service increase for this pair of stops, so the direct benefit is expected to be limited. [C-10]

**Takeaway.** Passenger benefits depend on the services people can catch and the stops they can reach. These comparisons use scheduled stop-to-stop journeys. Walking times at either end depend on the actual addresses. [C-08, C-09, C-10]

## 6. Who pays, and how much funding is in place?

The Australian Government, state and local governments, and operating agencies have different roles. The federal and Queensland governments each fund half of the southern rail upgrade, The Wave Stage 1 and Coomera Connector Stage 1. The audit office reports cumulative spending for the first two, and Queensland’s capital statement gives an estimated cumulative figure for Coomera Connector. The [funding table](data/brisbane-transport/stage-4-figures.md#財源表) gives the scope, dates and spending basis for all ten cases. [C-13]

Queensland's budget puts the general South East Queensland public transport subsidy at about A$2.787 billion in estimated spending for 2025–26 and A$2.946 billion budgeted for 2026–27. The Queensland Department of Transport and Main Roads (TMR) recorded about A$325 million in fare revenue in 2023–24, A$100 million in 2024–25 and A$88 million in 2025–26. The 50-cent fare moved from trial to permanent policy during this period. The state separately estimated a five-year fiscal cost of A$1.522 billion for permanent 50-cent fares from 2024–25 through 2028–29. Departmental fare revenue and the South East Queensland operating subsidy cover different scopes. They cannot be directly subtracted. [C-11]

**Takeaway.** For some projects, public records show who pays, the budget and spending to date. Queensland also budgets for general public transport operations in South East Queensland. The public figures do not show individual operating costs for M1 and M2 or annual maintenance spending for each service. [C-11, C-13]

## 7. What can we conclude so far?

The clearest evidence shows which services are operating, what timetables say and what published funding covers. Clayfield shows a measurable timetable difference between Wednesday and Saturday daytime direct trains, alongside nearby bus options. The project documents reviewed do not identify a specific Saturday service increase for Clayfield. [C-02, C-12]

**Takeaway.** Timetables and boarding records already show how services have changed. Whether new projects improve commutes from places like Clayfield will depend on published timetables and actual operating data. [C-12, C-13]
